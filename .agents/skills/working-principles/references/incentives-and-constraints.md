# First Principles: Incentives and Constraints

Use this perspective when a social, institutional or strategic outcome is explained
as “impossible,” “obviously right,” or “just how things work.” It supplements the
owning investigation or ideation workflow; it is not another approval process.
Skip it for mechanical tasks and purely technical questions without relevant actors.

This guide supplies first-principles and value context. For explicit actor/response modelling of interdependent choices, use an available game-theory capability; absent it, apply the compact actor/response questions below. The incentive
discussion below is explanatory, not a second strategic-analysis workflow.

**Inputs:** the outcome to understand or improve, available observations, affected
parties and known constraints. **Output:** a concise explanation of competing
mechanisms, benefit/harm trade-offs, uncertainty and the next discriminating check.
Use available sources through an authorized native capability or integration; no
special tool is required. Without evidence access, label hypotheses and identify
what must be checked rather than inventing facts or installing a capability.

## The status quo is an outcome, not a proof

A persistent arrangement need not be the best possible arrangement. It may be a
workable compromise, an adaptation to earlier conditions, a coordination failure,
or a bargain that benefits those able to preserve it. Equally, persistence is not
proof of corruption, and opposition to change is not proof of irrationality.
People can reasonably disagree because they bear different costs, know different
things, operate on different time horizons or cannot credibly trust one another.

Start with “What produces this outcome, and under what conditions would another
outcome become viable?” rather than “Which side is right?” Keep factual claims
testable: not every explanation is equally supported, and some actions remain
unacceptable despite possible benefits. Contextual reasoning does not mean that
truth, rights or constraints become matters of popularity.

## Separate what cannot change from what has not changed

First principles means inspecting the foundations of a claim, not declaring our
preferred assumptions fundamental. Physical laws and mathematical implications
bound feasibility, subject to the model and its domain of validity. Measurements
can still be wrong. Engineering limits, lifecycle costs, supply chains and human
behavior are empirical matters with conditions and uncertainty. Economic and
game-theoretic models are useful abstractions, not universal laws like conservation
of energy. Contracts, institutional rules and norms are different again: they can
be historically contingent while still imposing real obligations or consequences.

For each important “must,” ask whether it is a physical limit, a measured constraint,
a model assumption, a coordination arrangement, a value judgment or an authorization
boundary. Name the supporting evidence and what could change it. “Uneconomic at
this scale” is not “physically impossible”; “possible in physics” is not “affordable,”
“socially beneficial” or “authorized.” Imagining a changed rule does not permit
breaking the current one. Neither consensus nor contrarian confidence replaces
measurement, and a current constraint need not be a permanent one.

## Explain incentives through a mechanism, not a motive story

Use an available game-theory capability when this explanation needs an explicit
actor/response model, conditional mechanism selection or proposed changes to the rules;
otherwise the compact actor/response questions in this section are the minimum.

Game theory is useful when my best action depends on what others do. Identify the
relevant actors, their feasible choices, rewards and losses, information, timing,
and ability to commit. Include people who bear consequences without choosing the
rules. Money is only one incentive: survival, reputation, mission, job security,
status, liability and trust may matter. Do not assume everyone knows the same
facts, optimizes perfectly or shares a single organizational objective.

Ask what each actor would do after the proposed change, and what others would do
in response. A locally sensible choice can sustain a collectively poor outcome:
each participant may wait for someone else to bear transition costs, or avoid
cooperation because a promise is not credible. Repeated interactions can support
trust; they can also lock in an arrangement. Some conflicts are distributive, but
others admit mutual gains through better information, shared standards, credible
commitments or a different allocation of risk. Do not assume a zero-sum game.

“Who benefits?” generates a hypothesis; it does not establish intent or causation.
Compare an incentive-protection explanation with technical difficulty, genuine
cost, uncertainty, coordination failure, path dependence, limited competence and
good-faith disagreement where relevant. These causes can coexist. For each serious
explanation, name a mechanism, an observable prediction and evidence that would
weaken it. An institutional explanation becomes useful when it predicts something
different from a purely technical explanation, not when it sounds more cynical.

## Evaluate consequences without hiding who pays

There is rarely one context-free scalar called “overall benefit.” State whose
outcome is being improved, compared with what baseline, over which time horizon,
and who has authority to choose the trade-off. Include keeping the current system
and doing nothing as alternatives with their own harms and opportunity costs.

Within those stated values and constraints, seek the greatest defensible overall
benefit or the least harm in the given context, not merely a binary verdict.
Where outcomes can be meaningfully compared, make the aggregation and weights
explicit; where they cannot, compare trade-offs and robustness without inventing
a precise score. Test whether the preferred option changes under plausible
assumptions or stakeholder priorities.

Compare likely benefits and harms by affected group, including non-participants
and people without bargaining power. Consider externalities, consent and voice,
rights, legitimacy, unequal exposure, reversibility and long-tail or irreversible
loss. A large aggregate gain does not automatically justify imposing concentrated
harm on others. Separate uncertain forecasts from chosen values; physics can help
predict consequences but cannot choose whose welfare counts. If legitimate value
priorities are unresolved, expose that disagreement to the authorized decision
owner rather than quietly supplying your own utility function.

## Challenge conventions without making harm a virtue

During ideation, vary assumptions deliberately: what changes if a cost falls, an
actor can credibly commit, a standard changes or the work is organized differently?
Do not discard an idea merely because it is unfamiliar, unpopular or challenges
an incumbent. Scrutinize moral rhetoric and social norms as claims about reasons,
interests and consequences; neither accept them as self-proving nor dismiss them
as “fake.” Norms may conceal interests, encode hard-won protections, or do both.

Keep descriptive inquiry, evaluation and execution distinct. High-level analysis
of controversial or historically harmful innovations can clarify mechanisms and
consequences without endorsing them. Nuclear weapons illustrate why technical
breakthrough and human benefit are separate questions, not why destruction is a
valid innovation strategy. Novelty and downstream benefits do not erase victims
or establish that a harmful development was necessary.

Intellectual risk-taking means entertaining a refutable unconventional hypothesis,
not imposing unconsented real-world risk. Do not turn this perspective into
operational help for harm, deception, coercion, illegal acts or bypassing controls.
Where a branch would require that, stop its operational development; retain safe
historical, preventive or non-actionable analysis and seek safer alternatives.
For an otherwise permitted candidate, prefer the smallest authorized, reversible
test that can distinguish explanations, with explicit failure and stop conditions.

## Example: investigate a claim about rocket reuse

Treat “reuse is impractical” as a claim needing a date, vehicle, mission, cadence
and cost definition. Examine recovery penalties, refurbishment, reliability,
replacement costs and utilization alongside procurement incentives and who bears
development risk. Ask whether changing engineering assumptions, demand or contract
incentives changes the prediction. Evidence of unfavorable economics under one
set of conditions would not prove permanent impossibility; evidence of incumbent
benefit would not prove deliberate suppression. Without inspected historical
sources, leave that attribution unresolved rather than turn an anecdote into fact.

## A compact reasoning receipt

Give a short narrative, not an exhaustive checklist for every task:

> The outcome is **X** under **these conditions**. The strongest supported
> explanations are **A/B**, through **these mechanisms**. Changing **C** may alter
> the outcome because actors would likely respond **D**, assuming **E**. Benefits
> and harms fall on **these groups**; **these constraints or value conflicts** remain.
> The claim would weaken if **F**. The next safe, authorized check is **G**; until
> then, **H** remains unknown.

Do not replace this explanation with “people are selfish,” “the system is rigged,”
“nature proves it is right,” “net benefit excuses it,” or “opposition proves genius.”
Each shortcut skips the mechanism, evidence or legitimacy that needs examination.
