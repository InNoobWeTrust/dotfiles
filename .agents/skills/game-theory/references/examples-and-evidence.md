# Worked Cases and Evidence Limits

Use these cases to see the method, not to substitute a familiar story for current
evidence. A source-reported observation, an inferred mechanism and a synthetic
illustration are different kinds of support. None proves agent performance improved.

## Adoption despite shared eventual benefits

**Synthetic example.** Several teams like a shared platform, yet none adopts first.
Do not jump to “resistant to change.” The early team may bear migration, compatibility
and on-call costs before network benefits arrive, while each expects others to wait.
Alternatively, the platform may be unreliable or remove valued control.

Trace the proposal: a coordinated migration with credible support could reduce
first-mover exposure → early participation becomes less costly → other teams may
join. But an unfunded support promise or imposed uncompensated risk breaks that chain.
Compare actual reliability and capacity evidence with conditional willingness to adopt.
Seek an authorized, reversible pilot only if it distinguishes the explanations;
do not manufacture consent or treat the analyst's hypothesis as observed motivation.
This illustrates coordination, commitment and distribution, not a proven intervention.

## Daycare fines and transfer limits

**Reported field observation, not our experiment.** Gneezy and Rustichini (2000)
introduced a small monetary late-pickup fine in a group of Israeli daycare centers;
late pickups increased and did not fall after removal during the study period.
The result challenges the simple prediction that this fine would deter lateness.
It does not establish that fines generally increase unwanted behavior.

The authors discuss at least two interpretations: the fine supplied information
about an incomplete contract (a small specified consequence rather than an uncertain
one), or it reframed lateness as a purchasable service rather than a social violation.
The observed effect does not uniquely identify either mechanism; do not present
“crowding out morals” as a directly measured fact. Small sample, particular institutions,
amount, enforcement and time period matter to transfer.

The 2020 article *Is a fine still a price? Replication as robustness in empirical
legal studies* reports two MTurk vignette/survey studies where fines reduced stated
noncompliance, with little consistent crowding-out evidence. These are different-method
hypothetical studies, **not a replication of the original field intervention**.
They challenge generality and mechanism stories without erasing the field observation.

**Handback:** a price-like fine could alter perceived permission or consequences;
deterrence remains a rival conditional prediction. Check existing policy, actual
capacity to arrive on time, amount/enforcement and perceived meaning. What evidence
would distinguish changed beliefs from unchanged constraints or reporting? Larger or
more certain penalties might deter, but are not a universal remedy and can impose
unequal harm. Any real intervention needs consent, authority and domain-appropriate checks.

## Modernization that changes dependence

**Secondary account of empirical cases.** The Academy's 2009 public-information
article describes Nepal irrigation upgrades where durable dams reduced the need
for maintenance labor. Upstream users became less dependent on downstream users,
and incentives to share water changed; some schemes performed worse.

The useful question is not “modernization is bad,” but “which cooperative dependence
did this technical improvement remove?” Trace maintenance needs → bargaining position
→ allocation → downstream outcomes. Compare hydrology, engineering condition, allocation
rules and participation before inferring this mechanism in another project. An alternative
institution might preserve legitimate joint control without preserving an inefficient dam.
This source is a summary, not our inspection of original data or current local conditions.

## Boundary and unavailable-capability cases

**Actor-free technical case:** a hash computation is slow, with no relevant human
interdependence in the question. Skip strategic modeling; inspect measurements and
technical causes. A game-theory narrative would add noise, not common sense.

**Missing evidence/search:** a supplier says a requested warranty is impossible,
but no records or retrieval are available. Do not infer a hidden cartel. Possible
mechanisms include uncertain quality, limited capital, liability, technical limits
or reluctance to bear risk. Explain which terms/authority would have to change and
which evidence would discriminate. Use the short path or a bounded named mechanism
section without a catalog engine. Keep a conditional answer; do not install a tool,
invent a payoff matrix or claim to know the supplier's motives.

## Source map

These sources support conditional concepts, not certification of a strategy. Lectures
from one course share an origin; the Academy summaries describe underlying work,
not independent replications of every result.

### Strategic models

Ben Polak, Yale ECON159 (Fall 2007), **lecture overviews and chapter headings**, not
full transcripts: [1](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-1)
(players/payoffs, cooperation versus coordination),
[13](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-13)
(sequential choices, moral hazard/contracts),
[15](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-15)
(credibility of threats), [17](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-17)
(bargaining assumptions and wealth), [21](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-21)
(repetition and limits of cooperation), [23](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-23)
(communication, verifiability and signaling). Educational model exposition,
not a predictive validation on the reader's organization.

### Information economics

Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences,
[2001 public information](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2001/popular-information/),
full HTML; “Markets with Asymmetric Information” and Akerlof/Spence/Stiglitz sections.
Supports adverse selection, signaling and screening under model conditions. The
Akerlof section specifically discusses reciprocity toward employers and fairness
toward colleagues: motivations need not be monetary. Original cited research papers
were not inspected; no claim of complete coverage of identity or behavioral economics.

### Mechanism design

Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences,
[2007 public information](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2007/popular-information/),
full HTML; “Asymmetric information,” “Key concepts and results,” concluding remarks.
Incentive compatibility, model-bound institutional design, private information and
multiple equilibria. It does not establish that designers maximize public welfare,
that an equilibrium is unique, or that a proposed institution will work in practice.
Original technical papers were not inspected.

### Institutional evidence

Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences,
[2009 public information](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/popular-information/),
full HTML; commons, failed modernization, active participation and mutual dependence.
Ostrom's cases challenge inevitable commons failure; Williamson's account addresses
relationship-specific dependence and governance trade-offs. Summaries of empirical
and theoretical work, not original-data reanalysis. No universal governance recipe.

### Fine intervention and challenge

Gneezy and Rustichini (2000),
[*A Fine Is a Price*](https://doi.org/10.1086/468061), *Journal of Legal Studies* 29(1),
1–17: original full text inspected by a separate source-analysis worker during authoring.
Field observation and competing interpretations, not direct identification of one motive.

[*Is a fine still a price? Replication as robustness in empirical legal studies*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0144818819302741)
(2020): **abstract only** inspected; two MTurk survey/vignette studies, not a field
replication. Different setting/method limits the comparison. No additional original
datasets, practitioner interviews or field tests were performed for this skill.

The adoption, supplier and approval-rule examples are synthetic. Ticket-proxy gaming,
coalition-rule reconstruction and response tracing are practical analytical applications,
not validated causal claims about a particular institution. Inspect local evidence
before transferring them.
