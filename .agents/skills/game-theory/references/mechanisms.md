# Strategic Mechanisms

Read the sections selected by the [catalog](../assets/mechanisms.csv), not every pattern
on every task. These are conditional explanatory models, not diagnoses by analogy.
For each application, distinguish the documented rule or observation from the
assumed preferences, beliefs and response. Multiple mechanisms can operate together.
Source locators below point to the [evidence ledger](examples-and-evidence.md#source-map);
they do not imply that every practical suggestion has been empirically validated.

## Coordination

People may agree on a destination yet wait because moving alone is expensive.
For example, a team may prefer a shared standard once others adopt it, but prefer
its current tooling while nobody else has moved. More explanation of the standard's
eventual benefits does not remove that first-mover exposure.

Ask whether the blocker is uncertainty about participation, incompatible expectations,
or an actual incentive to defect. A shared schedule, compatible transition path or
conditional participation agreement may help coordination; it will not resolve every
distributional loss. Check resources and credible commitments before recommending them.

An equilibrium means no actor benefits from changing its own strategy alone, under
the model's assumptions. Several equilibria may exist. History, expectations and
institutions can affect which outcome occurs; writing down an attractive one does
not explain how people reach it. Predict whether credible evidence of others joining
would change behavior. If not, cost, control or conflicting interests may dominate.

**Basis:** Yale lecture 1 overview; model-based multiple-equilibrium caveat in
[mechanism design](examples-and-evidence.md#mechanism-design). No guaranteed selection rule.

## Cooperation

Distinguish coordination from a cooperation problem: here an actor might still gain
by withholding its contribution even after others contribute. Collective gains do
not by themselves make contribution individually attractive. Check who receives the
benefit, who bears effort and whether deviations are observable.

A valued future relationship can change today's incentives, but repeated interaction
is not magic. Observation errors, short horizons, turnover, weak enforcement or good
exit options can undermine cooperation. A threatened response must be feasible and
credible when the time comes; indiscriminate punishment can destroy trust instead.
Consider mutual assurance, clearer obligations or shared benefits before assuming
people need stronger penalties. Norms and intrinsic motivation can also matter.

**Check:** would changing future benefits or confidence in others' contributions alter
the action? If capacity is missing, assurance alone will not fix it. **Basis:** Yale
lectures 1 and 21 overviews in [strategic models](examples-and-evidence.md#strategic-models).

## Principal-agent

A person delegates work but cannot fully observe the other's effort or decisions.
The delegate may reasonably optimize a different objective, especially if it bears
costs the sponsor ignores. This hidden-action problem is often called moral hazard;
the name does not establish moral fault.

If a team is rewarded for tickets closed, faster closure might reflect better work,
smaller tickets, premature closure or changed reporting. Proxy gaming is a hypothesis
to investigate, not proof of fraud. Ask what the metric omits, what actions it makes
attractive, and who absorbs defects or risk. Adding another metric can create another
proxy rather than solve the problem. Consider observable outcomes, meaningful quality
checks and feasible responsibility/risk allocation; do not assume perfect monitoring.

**Check:** compare the proxy with independent outcome evidence and actual effort/capacity
constraints. Distinguish hidden actions after delegation from hidden quality before it.
**Basis:** Yale lecture 13 overview for moral hazard/contracts and the
[mechanism-design account](examples-and-evidence.md#mechanism-design) for incentive alignment.
The ticket example is a synthetic application, not an observed organizational finding.

## Information

Private information changes both choices and who participates. **Adverse selection**
concerns hidden preexisting quality or risk: terms attractive to one type of participant
may drive another away. It differs from hidden effort after agreement.

**Signaling** is an informed actor conveying information; **screening** is an uninformed
actor structuring choices or checks to learn it. Neither a confident claim nor an
expensive display guarantees truth. A costly signal may separate types only under
conditions such as different costs or benefits; everyone might afford the same display.
Cheap communication can still inform when interests align or claims are verifiable.

Ask who knows what, whether incentives favor honest disclosure, what can be verified
without guessing intent, and whether a proposed rule changes applicant/participant
composition. A warranty can convey information and allocate risk, but may also be
limited by liquidity, enforceability or genuine uncertainty. Treat those as rivals.
Use proportionate, authorized verification, not deceptive tests or invasive profiling.

**Basis:** [information economics](examples-and-evidence.md#information-economics),
Akerlof/Spence/Stiglitz sections; Yale lecture 23 overview. These conditional models
do not justify conclusions about a particular person's hidden qualities without evidence.

## Commitment

A sincere promise today may become unattractive tomorrow. Reconstruct the future
decision point: after the other party invests, delivers or becomes dependent, what
can the promisor actually do, and what will it then prefer? A threat may also be
unattractive to carry out. Stating it more forcefully does not make it credible.

Relationship-specific investment can create **hold-up** exposure if one party has few
alternatives after investing. Ask about enforceable agreements, staged obligations,
credible exit, verification and renegotiation. These are candidate safeguards, not
permission to impose terms, manufacture dependency or escalate threats. Commitments
can be expensive, inequitable or impossible to enforce; preserving flexibility may
sometimes be better. Never recommend irreversible self-harm as generic strategic advice.

**Check:** does the promise remain feasible and attractive at the relevant later node?
What if conditions change or enforcement fails? **Basis:** Yale lectures 13/15 overviews
and Williamson's mutual-dependence account in
[institutional evidence](examples-and-evidence.md#institutional-evidence).

## Bargaining

Agreement can create a surplus while disagreement persists over who gets it. Examine
credible outside options, switching costs, patience, liquidity, deadlines, uncertainty
and asymmetric dependence. A weaker party may accept a bad outcome because its
alternative is worse, not because it endorses the arrangement.

Separate expanding joint gains from distributing them. Terms that reduce transition
risk or clarify obligations may unlock agreement; merely urging compromise may not.
Do not assume a fifty-fifty split is predicted, fair or feasible. An attractive outside
option must be real, not a bluff supplied by the analyst. Include affected people who
are absent from negotiations: a bargain can be stable while shifting harm to them.

**Check:** would changed alternatives, timing or risk allocation change acceptable terms?
If parties still disagree, investigate beliefs, identity, legitimacy and non-negotiable
constraints. **Basis:** Yale lecture 17 overview (including wealth-effect caveat) and
Williamson in [institutional evidence](examples-and-evidence.md#institutional-evidence).

## Collective choice

“Most people support it” does not specify how a decision gets made. Reconstruct the
actual procedure: who proposes, sets the agenda, approves, vetoes, funds, implements
and can exit? Check whether informal influence differs from formal authority. Keep
each institutional claim attached to an accessible rule or observation, or mark it unknown.

If a hypothetical change needs three separate approvals, two supporters are not enough.
If it needs two of three votes, several coalitions may pass it, but execution may still
depend on someone else. These are transparent consequences of stated example rules,
not a general theorem about politics. Preferences can change when proposals are bundled,
timing changes, or implementation costs become visible. Do not assume a coalition is
unified internally or remains stable after its members obtain what they want.

**Check:** distinguish binding decision rights from a merely vocal objection; identify
which loss or dependency the blocking actor faces, plus legitimate ways to address it.
Do not suggest bypassing controls, collusion or coercion. **Basis:** decision-rule
reconstruction and synthetic examples, not a verified coalition/agenda-setting model.
Use actual institutional evidence; absent rules block a confident political prediction.

## Institutions and commons

Shared benefits and individually costly contributions can create free-riding; scarce
shared resources can invite overuse. Neither problem proves inevitable collapse or
that privatization, central control or community governance is always best.

Inspect who may use the resource, how allocation and maintenance obligations interact,
who participates in rule changes, what can be monitored, whether enforcement is
legitimate and feasible, and how disputes can be resolved. Do local rules match actual
conditions? Are weaker/non-participating groups exposed? These are conditional design
questions informed by cases, not a universal checklist that guarantees cooperation.

A technical upgrade may remove a maintenance burden that previously sustained mutual
dependence. Ask what social function the old inconvenience served before removing it;
preserve useful cooperation through legitimate institutions, not needless inefficiency
or forced dependence. Compare engineering, resource and governance explanations.

**Basis:** Ostrom commons/active-participation and Nepal failed-modernization accounts
in [institutional evidence](examples-and-evidence.md#institutional-evidence), secondary
summaries of empirical studies, not original-data verification or universal prescriptions.

## Feedback and norms

People can care about fairness, identity, reciprocity and doing good work, not just
material rewards. Norms can protect cooperation, preserve exclusion or do both.
Their causal role and their desirability are separate questions.

A new payment or penalty can change the meaning of an interaction, beliefs about
what is permitted, who joins, and later expectations. A fine might deter behavior;
it might instead be treated as a price. Either prediction depends on context. Trace
the changed belief or constraint rather than asserting that incentives always backfire
or that every social response is rational optimization. Habit, limited attention,
learning, genuine capacity limits and measurement changes are alternative causes.

**Check:** distinguish changes in behavior, composition and reporting; seek evidence
of the proposed mechanism, persistence after removal and a comparison condition.
Feedback loops and proxy-targeting effects are practical causal hypotheses, not laws
established by a single anecdote. **Basis:** specific Akerlof reciprocity/fairness passage
in [information economics](examples-and-evidence.md#information-economics), and
[the daycare case](examples-and-evidence.md#daycare-fines-and-transfer-limits).
